Foundational / 01.04

Value before detail

Foundational · Lesson 4 of 9
45–90 minutes; may be split across two sessions. Prerequisite: the previous lesson in this category; if the foundational concepts are unclear, return to lessons 01–03.

The problem this lesson addresses

Compressing the picture into light, middle and dark groups checks whether subject and background separate. Detail can hide structural problems, so squint or shrink the image first, then decide where edges must be crisp.

Goal: complete the specific exercise of “Value before detail” and be able to say when the method helps and where it falls short. Write a prediction in your own words before you start working or analyzing.

Example: what a choice leads to

A complex street scene shrunk to a thumbnail may crush signage and figures into one dark mass; finely painted parts still fail to create hierarchy.

This is an original teaching scenario, not a citation of historical fact. Keep three layers separate in your notes: what actually appears in the material; what you infer from it; and what further sources you would need to judge further.

Screen lab: the same gray

The two center grays have identical values; different backgrounds may change how they look.

Predict first which one seems lighter, then adjust the background. This demonstrates adjacency; it does not simulate paint, exposure or real material.

Work through the steps

  1. Prepare: start from existing material. Write down the one variable you will change today and the conditions held constant; attempt it once without looking at hints first.
  2. Compare: keep at least two outcomes as the assignment requires and record the differences item by item. Do not keep only the best one, or you cannot tell what caused the change.
  3. Verify: check your observations against this lesson's concept; find one original work or demonstration in the resource portal and point out how your model differs from the original's qualities.
  4. Redo: choose one specific deviation and do it again; redo with a different object a week later to test whether the method transfers.

Assignment for this lesson

Paint six three-value thumbnails with a fixed subject while changing the areas of light and dark; pick one and explain where the eye lands first.

Feedback: complete first, then expand

The key acceptance check for this lesson

The six are not detail variations but genuinely different value structures.

If it is not met, shrink the assignment first, then add a comparison set. If it is met, repeat the method with a different object; a different result need not be an error — check background and conditions first.

Common pitfalls and corrections

Pitfall one: reciting a term without showing how it maps onto the work. Correction: pair every term with one piece of locatable evidence.

Pitfall two: letting authorial intent override the actual effect. Correction: record what actually happened in viewing or testing, then explain the gap.

Pitfall three: treating one success as mastery. Correction: redo with a different object a week later, and keep the failed results.

Review and discussion

Recite one method and one limitation from memory the next day; try again with a different object on day seven. Send the lesson name, your assignment and your least certain judgment to your teacher for item-by-item feedback. Self-checks do not replace human review of open answers.
