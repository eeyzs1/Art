LESSON 10.01

What Does the Character Want

Lesson 1 of 9 · 15–25 minutes
Goal of this lesson: use the method of "What Does the Character Want" to add one more evidence-based observation of a work.

01 · Understand one concept

Dramatic action can be observed through a character's goal, obstacle, and tactics. Beyond the spoken lines, pauses, blocking, and responses also carry the relations between people. A character is not reducible to a single personality label.

Reading anchor: V&A: Theatre and Performance collection. Watch the collaborative process through costume, stage design, and performance archives; this British collection is no substitute for specialized material on Chinese xiqu. The everyday examples and exercises below are an original teaching design, not that institution's own text or authoritative conclusions.

02 · Study one example

"Stay" can be a plea or a command; the other person's reaction makes the relation clearer.

One line, two versions

Original example: "You finally came."

Version one: waiting at the door, stepping forward quickly

The action can support a reading of anticipation or welcome, but the surrounding context must still be checked.

Version two: back to the door, a pause, then said quietly

The same line may be read as disappointment, doubt, or restraint. Point to the evidence in the action and keep the other readings open.

Try changing only one verb or pause, and predict how the understanding changes.

03 · Do one exercise

Say "open the door" as a request, as a threat, and as a probe, and compare the pauses.

Open after finishing: check your approach

Check three things: whether you completed the assigned comparison; whether you pointed to specific shapes, movements, words, or sounds; whether you kept facts and interpretation apart. Your answer need not match the impressions of the example, as long as you can give grounds.

Check especially in this lesson: Goal and obstacle are an effective entry into stage action.
